1.2 · Design principles — apply across the app

  • Warm-severity verdict triad — never clinical red. NAFLD / MASH users catastrophize («fatty liver → cirrhosis → cancer»). Severity reads teal → amber → clay, never a stoplight red.
  • Night mode = the AI Coach surface. The 2am-anxious user — Nick after a scary lab, Robert checking his recovery — gets a calm dark Coach automatically 22:00–06:00.
  • Hopeful, non-judgmental framing. Especially for Robert: alcohol-related liver disease is stigmatised and often hidden from family. Use «recovery», «healing», «rebuild» — never «damage» or «abuse».
  • Every AI Coach answer cites a source — AASLD / AGA / EASL / Mayo Clinic. No citation = refusal pivot.
  • No diagnosis language. «Your ALT 85 maps to the elevated range per AASLD», never «you have NASH».
  • Plain-English labs. Every ALT / AST / GGT / FIB-4 value gets a verdict pill plus a one-line explanation a non-clinician understands.
  • Liver Score is a trend, not a verdict. Always show the 6-month direction — ALT lags weight loss by months, so a single absolute number demotivates.
  • Privacy-first. Labs and food photos stay on-device by default; iCloud sync is opt-in. RAG runs on-device (sqlite-vec).

6.1 · The engagement problem

A liver app has a structural retention problem before a line of code is written: the biology is too slow to feel. A blood panel moves once a quarter; ALT does not visibly change between Tuesday and Wednesday. The loop's whole job is to manufacture honest daily momentum across the 90-day gap between draws.

The failure mode — a quarterly utility. If Hepatica only surfaces lab data, the user opens it the day results land and forgets it for three months — that is a utility, not a habit, and it churns before the annual renewal. LiverPal shipped exactly that: a generic feed with no daily reason to return — 4 reviews, no traction. Hepatica must give the user something real to do each day and something visible to watch grow between blood tests, without faking biology or making health claims.

7.3 · Screenshot anti-patterns

Hard-lines from COMPLIANCE.md + SCREENSHOTS_GUIDE.md. Any one of these fails App Review or breaks the Health & Fitness (not Medical) positioning.

  • "Cure / reverse your fatty liver" as a promise — banned. "Reverse fatty liver" lives only as the brand tagline, never beside a date or a guaranteed outcome. Use "track", "understand", "manage".
  • "You have NAFLD" / diagnosis copy in a headline or bullet — banned. Use "consistent with elevated liver enzymes" · "ALT 85 — elevated per the AASLD threshold of 40".
  • FibroScan or FIB-4 staging without a visible educational disclaimer — banned. "FIB-4 suggests intermediate risk — discuss with your doctor", never "FIB-4 confirms F3 fibrosis".
  • "Rezdiffra is working" / "Ozempic is working" as a definitive claim — soften: the chart shows the user's own logged data, observed, not promised.
  • Real or identifiable patient data — seeded plausible values only (§7.0). No real faces, no faces in food photos, no "User: John Smith".
  • "#1 Fatty Liver App" / "best" / asterisk claims in a bullet — Apple 2.3.7 + unproven superiority. Use "evidence-based" or a specific, true feature claim.
  • Relapse-shame on frame 3 — no broken-streak red, no "you failed". A slip is logged honestly and framed non-judgmentally.
  • Clinical stoplight-red severity — kills warmth for fear-prone NAFLD users. Use the locked warm verdict triad (teal / amber / clay).
  • More than three bullets, or bullets over six words — a crowded frame reads as wallpaper. One headline, ≤3 bullets, one hero element.
  • Stock "detox" imagery — no glowing livers, no green detox juices. The category is clinical authority, not wellness — illustrate with real UI.
